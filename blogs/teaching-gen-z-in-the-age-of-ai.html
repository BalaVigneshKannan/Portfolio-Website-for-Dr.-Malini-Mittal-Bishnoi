Teaching Gen Z in the Age of AI

AI hasn't changed what my students need to learn. It has changed what's worth teaching them at all.

A few semesters ago, a student turned in an essay that was fluent, well-structured, and completely hollow. Every sentence was correct. Nothing in it was hers. That assignment taught me more about teaching in the age of AI than any workshop on "AI in the classroom" ever has: the problem was never going to be whether students could produce polished text. It was going to be whether they still had a reason to think.

My students arrived at university already fluent in tools I never had — able to summarize a reading, generate a counterargument, or polish a paragraph in seconds. Treating that as a threat to police felt both futile and beside the point. The more useful question was: what can I ask of them now that a machine can do the easy parts?

The answer, for my sociology seminars, has been to push harder on exactly the things AI is bad at: sitting with a contradiction instead of resolving it too quickly, noticing what a dataset leaves out, defending an argument out loud when someone pushes back on it in real time. Fieldwork before theory has always been my teaching philosophy — send students out to observe their own neighborhoods before handing them a framework to explain it. That principle has only become more important, not less. A chatbot can explain migration theory. It cannot walk through a labor camp and tell you what it noticed that the theory missed.

I've also stopped treating AI as something to hide from. We talk about it openly — where it helps, where it flattens, where it quietly launders someone else's assumptions into a student's "own" argument. Gen Z didn't ask to inherit these tools any more than my generation asked to inherit the internet. What they need from us isn't a ban. It's practice telling the difference between a shortcut and a substitute for thinking.

That, more than any policy on my syllabus, is the actual lesson.
