Fieldwork • Research • Ethnography

Notes from the Field

Good ethnography isn't about asking the right questions. It's about earning the right to be told the answer.

The first week of fieldwork is always the same: nobody tells you anything true. Not because people are hiding something dramatic, but because a stranger with a notebook hasn't yet earned the ordinary, unguarded version of their day. You get the version people give to inspectors, journalists, and anyone else who might use their words against them. That version is useful — it tells you what people think an outsider wants to hear — but it isn't the story.

The real material shows up later, usually sideways. Someone mentions, almost as an afterthought while pouring tea, the real reason they took a particular job. A throwaway comment about a phone call home reveals more about the emotional cost of migration than a formal interview ever could. You learn to stop treating the interview as the unit of data and start treating the relationship as the method.

That takes time most research timelines don't budget for. It also takes discomfort most methodology chapters don't mention: sitting through long silences, being visibly out of place, getting it wrong in ways that make people laugh at you a little before they trust you. I've had fieldwork days that produced nothing quotable and everything important — the day someone finally stopped performing for me and just talked.

What I try to pass on to my graduate students is less a technique than a posture: go in prepared to be the least informed person in the room, and stay there long enough for that to become an asset instead of a liability. The people you're studying are the experts on their own lives. Your job is not to extract their knowledge efficiently. It's to become someone worth telling it to.
