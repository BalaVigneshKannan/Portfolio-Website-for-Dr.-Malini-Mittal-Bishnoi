Migration • Labor • Storytelling

Why Migration Stories Matter

Behind every statistic on labor migration is a person with a name, a family, and a reason for leaving home.

When people talk about migration in the Gulf, they usually reach for numbers first: how many workers, from which countries, in which sectors. Numbers matter — they tell us the scale of something that shapes millions of lives. But numbers alone flatten people into categories. They can't tell you why a woman left her two children with her mother to clean houses six thousand kilometres away, or what it felt like the first time she called home and heard her daughter's voice sound a little more grown up than she remembered.

This is the gap I kept running into while researching Migrant Memoirs from the Middle East. The policy reports were thorough. The labor statistics were rigorous. But none of them could explain the quiet calculus a pink-collar worker makes every single day — weighing homesickness against opportunity, dignity against necessity, the employer who pays late against the fear of finding no employer at all.

Oral histories fill that gap. When someone tells you their own story, in their own words, you stop seeing a labor category and start seeing a strategy — a person actively making decisions inside a system that rarely asks for their input. That shift, from statistic to strategist, is not a small thing. It changes how we design policy, how we talk about "unskilled" labor, and how we understand the people who quite literally build and run the cities we live in.

I don't think storytelling replaces data. I think it's what data needs in order to mean something. A spreadsheet can tell you that domestic workers make up a significant share of migrant labor in the region. A conversation can tell you what it costs a person to be counted that way — and what it would take to count them differently.

That's the work I keep returning to: not just recording numbers, but making room for the story underneath them.
