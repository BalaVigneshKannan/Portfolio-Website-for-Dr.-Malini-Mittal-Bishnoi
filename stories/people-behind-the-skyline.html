People Behind the Skyline

Every tower has a name on the deed and a thousand names that built it. Only one of those usually makes the postcard.

A skyline photograph flattens everything into silhouette. It cannot show you the site engineer from Kerala who has sent three children through university on a salary earned pouring concrete in June heat, or the Filipina nurse whose shift ends as the office towers she can see from her window empty out for the night. The Gulf’s built environment is, quite literally, a monument to migration — yet migration rarely gets billed as the main character in how these cities are described.

My research among what are often called “pink-collar” and blue-collar migrant communities keeps returning to the same finding: people build far more than buildings. They build remittance economies that support entire towns back home. They build informal support networks — a countryman who knows a doctor, a roommate who can lend until payday — that function as a parallel social safety net. They build identities that stretch across two or three countries at once, never fully settled in either.

What gets lost in headline narratives about migration — numbers, quotas, remittance totals — is the texture of daily life underneath them. The Friday gatherings in a labour camp courtyard that recreate, almost exactly, a wedding tradition from home. The small grocery stall that stocks one specific brand of tea because enough homesick customers asked for it. These are not footnotes to the migration story. They are the story.

Sociology’s job, as I understand it, is to put people back into structures that tend to erase them — to insist that “labour force” is also a father, a daughter, a person with a Friday routine and an opinion about the neighbourhood mosque’s new imam. It is slower, less photogenic work than counting cranes on a skyline. But it is the only way to understand what a city actually is, rather than what it looks like from the observation deck.

The people behind the skyline rarely appear in the photograph. This is, in the end, simply an argument for looking somewhere else.
