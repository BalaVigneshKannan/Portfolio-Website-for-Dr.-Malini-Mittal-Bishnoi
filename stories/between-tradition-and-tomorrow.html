Gulf • Identity • Modernity

Between Tradition & Tomorrow

The Gulf is often framed as a tug-of-war between old and new. Fieldwork suggests it is something closer to a conversation.

Visitors often arrive with a question already half-formed: how does a region hold on to tradition while building so fast toward the future? It is a fair question, but it assumes tradition and modernity sit on opposite ends of a single line, with every society forced to choose a point along it. Two decades of fieldwork across the Gulf have convinced me that’s the wrong shape for the question entirely.

Consider the majlis — a centuries-old institution of gathering, hospitality and dispute resolution — which today coexists comfortably with WhatsApp groups that organise, announce and sometimes replace it. Or the young Emirati professional who prays five times a day and negotiates venture capital in the same afternoon, without experiencing either as a contradiction. These are not compromises between two competing worlds. They are evidence that “tradition” and “modernity” were never really separate categories for the people living them — only for outside observers trying to categorise from a distance.

What is genuinely changing is not whether tradition survives, but who gets to define it, and how quickly that definition can shift generation to generation. Grandparents and grandchildren in the same household often hold different ideas of what “traditional” even means — not because one is right and the other has drifted, but because tradition itself has always been a living, negotiated thing, not a fixed inheritance.

This matters for how the region tells its own story. Framing the Gulf as caught between sand and skyline makes for a tidy metaphor, but it flattens a much more interesting reality: a society actively authoring what continuity looks like on its own terms, at a pace few other places have had to manage.

The mosque and the mall exist a few kilometres apart, sometimes in the same skyline photograph. The more useful question isn’t which one represents the “real” Gulf — it’s what it means that both do, comfortably, to the people who live between them.
