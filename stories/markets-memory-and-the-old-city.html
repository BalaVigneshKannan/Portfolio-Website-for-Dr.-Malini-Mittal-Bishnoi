Heritage • Everyday Life • Belonging

Markets, Memory and the Old City

Long before air conditioning and glass towers, the souk was already doing the work a city needs most: holding people together.

Walk into any of the old souks that still operate along the Gulf’s original trading creeks, and you notice something the newer malls, for all their polish, rarely manage: strangers talk to each other. A spice seller quizzes a customer on what dish she’s cooking before recommending a blend. A textile trader remembers a regular’s daughter’s wedding measurements from three years ago. These are small interactions, but they are the raw material of what sociologists call “social memory” — the accumulated, shared knowledge that makes a place feel like somewhere rather than just a set of coordinates.

The old city districts carry this memory in their layout as much as in their transactions. Narrow sikkas designed for shade and foot traffic, not cars. Buildings positioned to catch a sea breeze before anyone had heard of climate control. Wind towers that solved, elegantly, a problem the region still lives with today. None of this is nostalgia for its own sake — it is evidence of a society that has always been adapting cleverly to its environment, long before “sustainability” became a marketing term.

What interests me as a researcher is how these old districts function today, alongside the new city rather than in opposition to it. They are not museums. Shoppers still haggle over gold weight and spice quantity in exactly the rhythms their grandparents used. Tourists photograph the same alleyways that longtime residents use for their weekly errands, two different relationships to the same physical space, coexisting without much friction.

Heritage, in this sense, is not something the Gulf preserves in spite of its modernity — it is something the region continues to use. The souk is not a relic being protected from disappearance. It is a working institution that has simply adapted its customers rather than its character.

Memory, in a fast-changing city, needs a place to live. In the old quarters, it still has one — with the shutters open for business.
